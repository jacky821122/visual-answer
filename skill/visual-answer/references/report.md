# report — data, numbers, specs

The reader doesn't want the data; they want what the data says about them and what to do
with it. The data is evidence for the story, not the story.

## Principles
- **Find the pattern before drawing anything.** Group items into categories that mean
  something to the reader (tiers, types, behaviours), and write one interpretive line per group.
  Ranked lists of everything are rarely the answer.
- **Lead with the insight and a focal point**: the one number or fact that best captures it, big.
  Then the 3–7 observations that matter, each a bold claim followed by its evidence.
- **Use the reader's own context.** Their remarks, goals and history explain the numbers; place
  each remark next to the item it explains and fold them into the observations. Raw numbers the
  reader has told you are misleading should be presented with that context, not "corrected".
- **Colour by meaning.** Each category gets its own colour, used for its bars, numbers, chips and
  heading marker. Highlighting = a category colour, never "accent vs grey sea".
- **Close with the so-what**: what this suggests they do, change or watch.
- **Specs and inventories**: speak as a knowledgeable friend reviewing the owner's thing —
  product names in plain language, a short summary of what matters, then practical notes the
  owner can act on. Raw query fields stay out.
- Charts: load `dataviz`. Derived metric → define it once in plain words near the chart.
  Outliers that would squash the scale: cap the axis and label the true value.
- Full tables go in `<details>` unless lookup is the point. Data-quality notes: footer.

## Examples (not required)
- An observations list near the top: bold claim, then the evidence, in one or two lines each.
- A small multiples row when several groups share one scale.

## Common mistakes
- Opening with medians, counts or a missing-data warning.
- Several charts of the same ranking; a full-length table on the main page.
- Exposing field names or match scores.
